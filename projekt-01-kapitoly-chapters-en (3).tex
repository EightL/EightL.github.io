\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}
Reinforcement learning is about agents learning to act in an environment purely from experience. \emph{Multi-agent reinforcement learning} (MARL) extends this to settings where multiple agents share the same environment, which makes everything harder, because the joint action space grows exponentially, credit assignment across agents becomes difficult, and the environment turns non-stationary as everyone learns at once~\cite{zhang2021, albrecht2024marl}.

But these difficulties are also what make MARL interesting for studying coordination. In a small heterogeneous team, one of the questions is whether agents settle into stable coordination regimes: who protects whom, how risk gets distributed, and what role spatial structure plays in all of it. Think of a football match, where human players rely on communication and predefined roles, but what happens when RL agents have only local observations and reward feedback? What kinds of structures can emerge, and how do you measure them without over-interpreting reward?

This thesis investigates these questions in Knights, Archers, and Zombies (KAZ), a cooperative environment where two knights and two archers must coordinate to survive increasingly difficult waves of enemies. We review reinforcement learning from tabular methods to multi-agent algorithms, then build a custom KAZ training pipeline using MAPPO, implemented in JAX (a numerical computing library that compiles Python code for GPU execution), with multiple parameter-sharing paradigms. Seven reward schemes are compared through a focused metric suite that measures how agents coordinate, how roles depend on each other, and whether grouping behavior actually emerges. The resulting coordination regimes are then tested under changes in observation range, architecture, and environment mechanics.

The main finding is that reward design does not just shift performance up or down. Different reward functions produce genuinely different coordination regimes -- a dense mixed-role cluster, a separated frontline-backline formation, an aggressive hybrid -- each with its own spatial signature and failure modes. Intuitive coordination proxies like focus fire, behavioral diversity, and spatial cohesion turn out to describe \emph{which} regime a policy occupies rather than how well it actually performs. Across all regimes, the strongest predictor of team durability is role-protective behavior, specifically knight survival, though this dependence weakens once archers can also break shields. An environment modification and a leave-one-out ablation support this more strongly than correlation alone.

With only four agents of two types, the team is too small for nontrivial coalition partitioning in the game-theoretic sense. Throughout this thesis, ``coalition'' refers to a group of agents that coordinates in space, timing, or contribution toward a shared sub-goal, consistent with the cooperative MARL literature.

The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:reinforcement_learning} introduces single-agent reinforcement learning. Chapter~\ref{chap:deep_rl} extends these ideas to deep RL with function approximation. Chapter~\ref{chap:marl} covers multi-agent reinforcement learning, MAPPO, and emergent coordination. Chapter~\ref{chap:environment} describes the environment. Chapter~\ref{chap:metrics} defines the metrics used to measure coordination regimes. Chapters~\ref{chap:training} and~\ref{chap:analysis} present the training experiments and their analysis. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} concludes with limitations and directions for future work.


\chapter{Reinforcement Learning}
\label{chap:reinforcement_learning}

Reinforcement learning is learning what to do in order to maximize a numerical reward signal. RL starts with a complete, interactive, goal-seeking agent, rather than focusing on isolated subproblems. It considers the problem of an agent interacting with an uncertain environment~\cite{sutton}.

    \caption[The agent--environment interaction in RL]{The agent--environment interaction in reinforcement learning: the agent selects action $A_t$, and the environment returns reward $R_t$ and next state $S_{t+1}$.}
    \label{fig:agent-env-interaction}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Distinguishing Features in Reinforcement Learning}\mbox{}\\
What makes reinforcement learning so interesting is that, unlike supervised learning, the learner is \emph{not told which actions to take} beforehand, but instead must \emph{discover} which actions yield the most reward by trying them~\cite{sutton}. A cat image classifier must train on thousands of images labeled as `cat' or `not cat', whereas if we want to teach AI to be good at chess, instead of labeling every possible combination of moves (which is practically impossible), an RL agent simply plays games and learns from wins and losses. 
RL agents are not provided a clear ``correct answer'', only environmental feedback indicating how good/bad an action was~\cite{sutton}. This parallels how humans learn from experience. A child learning to ride a bike receives immediate feedback through balance rather than explicit instructions for every muscle movement. In multi-agent settings, we cannot rely on supervised approaches, because optimal joint behaviors aren't known in advance~\cite{zhang2021}, so to perform optimally, agents must adapt to the environment and discover all sorts of tactical behavior just through experience.

\paragraph{Key Challenges in RL}\mbox{}\\
Consider an agent in a maze. It tries out sequences of actions and eventually finds the exit. But should it keep exploiting that known path, or explore further for a better one? This is the \emph{exploration-exploitation dilemma} (Figure~\ref{fig:exploit-explore})~\cite{sutton}. Each action also changes the state of the environment, affecting what future actions become available. Rewards are often sparse, meaning the agent may go many steps with no signal at all, which creates the \emph{temporal credit assignment problem}, where the agent might take a detrimental action at step $t=0$ but only receive a reward at $t=100$~\cite{sutton}. In a cooperative game, for instance, good formation and positioning early on could yield more reward in the long run.

\section{Markov Decision Process}
To define a framework in which agents operate, we formalize this with Markov Decision Processes (MDPs). An \emph{MDP} is a tuple \(\langle \mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma \rangle\) where \(\mathcal{S}\) is the state space, \(\mathcal{A}\) is the action space, \(P(s'|s,a)\) is the transition probability, \(R(s,a)\) is the reward function, and \(\gamma \in [0,1]\) is the discount factor~\cite{sutton}. At each step, the agent receives a representation of the environment's state \(S_t \in \mathcal{S}\), selects an action \(A_t \in \mathcal{A}\), and receives a reward \(R_t\) and a new state \(S_{t+1}\) as consequence. The agent's sole objective is to learn a policy \(\pi(a|s)\) that maximizes expected cumulative discounted reward~\cite{sutton}:
\[
  \mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t R_t\right]
\]
The key assumption here is that all future states depend only on current \(S_t\) and \(A_t\), not on the history of past states; this is known as the \emph{Markov Property}~\cite{sutton}. Everything outside the agent's direct control, including other agents' behaviors, is considered part of the environment. However, in multi-agent settings with centralized training (CTDE discussed in Section~\ref{ctde}), critics might have access to privileged joint state information during learning.

\section{Value Functions and Policies}
An RL agent needs a way to measure how good a situation is, and a rule for what to do next. Therefore, this section defines the foundations of every algorithm in reinforcement learning.

\paragraph{Returns and Discounting}\mbox{}\\
Return $G_t$ is the sum of future rewards an agent expects to receive from time step $t$ onward~\cite{spinningup2018}. In episodic tasks, this can be the finite-horizon undiscounted return $G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{T-t-1} R_{t+k}$, where $T$ is the terminal time step~\cite{spinningup2018}. The discount factor ($\gamma$) is fundamental, because it has a strong influence on the agent's behavior in multi-agent settings (illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:discount-long-term}), discussed later in Section~\ref{subsec:reward}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{obrazky-figures/discount_long_term.pdf}
    \caption[Discount Factor and Long-Term Planning]{Effect of discounting on agent behaviour in KAZ. Left: the agent targets a nearby zombie for an immediate reward of $+1$. Right: the agent instead commits to pursuing the boss, earning $+\gamma^t \times 3$ at a future time step $t$. A low $\gamma$ makes the agent prefer the immediate zombie kill; a high $\gamma$ makes the harder, delayed boss kill worthwhile.}
    \label{fig:discount-long-term}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Value Functions}\mbox{}\\
Value functions estimate expected returns from states or state-action pairs under a policy~\cite{spinningup2018}. The \emph{state-value function} $V^{\pi}(s) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[G_t | S_t = s]$ gives the expected return from state $s$ following policy $\pi$~\cite{spinningup2018}. The \emph{action-value function} $Q^{\pi}(s,a) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[G_t | S_t = s, A_t = a]$ gives the expected return from taking action $a$ in state $s$, then following $\pi$~\cite{spinningup2018}. These satisfy the \emph{Bellman equations}, expressing that a state's value equals immediate reward plus discounted next-state value~\cite{sutton, spinningup2018}. In MARL, individual value functions require credit assignment to decompose global rewards~\cite{zhang2021}.

Since $V^\pi$ and $Q^\pi$ depend on the policy, different policies produce different value functions. The \emph{optimal value function} $V^*(s) = \max_\pi V^\pi(s)$ gives the highest achievable expected return from each state, taken over all possible policies~\cite{sutton}. Any policy that achieves $V^*$ everywhere is an \emph{optimal policy} $\pi^*$, as illustrated for a simple grid game in Figure~\ref{fig:optimal-value-policy}. Finding $\pi^*$ (or a good approximation of it) is the central objective of reinforcement learning~\cite{sutton}.

\paragraph{Policy Representation}\mbox{}\\
A policy $\pi$ maps states to actions~\cite{spinningup2018}. Policies can be deterministic ($\mu: \mathcal{S} \rightarrow \mathcal{A}$) or stochastic ($\pi: \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{A} \rightarrow [0,1]$)~\cite{spinningup2018}. Discrete action spaces use categorical policies with softmax outputs, while continuous spaces use diagonal Gaussian policies~\cite{spinningup2018}. MARL agents may share parameters or maintain separate policies~\cite{zhang2021, hu2022policy}.

\section{Tabular Solution Methods}
In an ideal world, where we know exactly every transition probability $P(s'|s,a)$ and reward function $R(s,a)$, we could compute optimal policies using Dynamic Programming. However, DP requires complete knowledge of the environment and scales poorly as state spaces grow~\cite{sutton}. For practical RL, we must instead rely on \emph{model-free methods} that learn from trial-and-error interaction.

\paragraph{Monte Carlo Methods}\mbox{}\\
When the environment's model is unknown, the agents have to estimate values by sampling their trajectories~\cite{sutton}. This leads us to Monte Carlo methods that solve this by letting the agent play out the entire episodes from start to finish and using the actual empirical return $G_t$ to update the values of the states visited~\cite{sutton}. The update rule simply shifts the current value estimate towards the observed return:
\[
  V(S_t) \leftarrow V(S_t) + \alpha\bigl[G_t - V(S_t)\bigr],
\]
where $\alpha$ is a constant step-size parameter or learning rate~\cite{sutton}.
Because MC methods do not \emph{bootstrap} -- meaning they do not rely on other learned value estimates to update their current one -- their value estimates are entirely unbiased~\cite{sutton}. However, because $G_t$ tracks every single random choice over a long period, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:monte-carlo}, MC updates suffer from severely high variance~\cite{sutton}. In addition, the agent has to wait until the end of the episode before it can make any learning updates~\cite{sutton}. That is where TD-learning is introduced.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{obrazky-figures/monte-carlo.drawio.pdf}
    \caption[Monte Carlo]{Abstract visualization of how the return $G_t$ is computed.}
    \label{fig:monte-carlo}
\end{figure}


\paragraph{TD-Learning}\mbox{}\\
Temporal-Difference (TD) learning resolves the problem of waiting until the episode terminates by bootstrapping~\cite{sutton}. What bootstrapping does is that it substitutes the true return by updating value estimates \emph{online after every single step}~\cite{sutton}. It uses the immediate reward and the current value estimate of the next state to form a proxy for the actual return~\cite{sutton}. The simplest variant, known as TD(0), updates the state-value function as follows:
\[
  V(S_t) \leftarrow V(S_t) + \alpha \underbrace{\Bigl[
    \underbrace{R_t + \gamma\,V(S_{t+1})}_{\text{TD target}}
    \;-\; V(S_t)
  \Bigr]}_{\delta_t\ (\text{TD error})}
\]
where $\alpha$ is the learning rate. The TD error $\delta_t$, seen in Figure~\ref{fig:td-learning}, is the continuous correction signal, measuring the difference between what the agent predicted and what it actually observed one step later~\cite{sutton}. When $\delta_t > 0$, the outcome was better than predicted, so the value estimate is nudged upward, and when $\delta_t < 0$, the reverse applies~\cite{sutton}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{obrazky-figures/TD-learning.drawio.pdf}
    \caption[TD-Learning]{TD error $\delta_t$ computed from just two consecutive states (green). Unlike Monte Carlo, the rest of the episode (grey) is not needed.}
    \label{fig:td-learning}
\end{figure}


\paragraph{Q-Learning}\mbox{}\\
Now Q-Learning extends TD-learning where instead of simply evaluating policies, it \emph{directly finds optimal actions} by learning the action-value function $Q(s,a)$~\cite{sutton}. Q-learning applies the following update:
\[
  Q(S_t,A_t) \leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t)
  + \alpha\Bigl[R_t + \gamma
  \underbrace{\max_{a'} Q(S_{t+1},a')}_{\text{greedy next value}}
  - \ Q(S_t,A_t)\Bigr].
\]
The $\max$ operator evaluates the best possible action, assuming the agent will act optimally from the next state onward~\cite{sutton}. Q-learning is strictly \emph{off-policy} because it always evaluates the greedy (optimal) target policy, regardless of which behavior policy the agent actually follows for exploration. This separation allows the agent to explore freely while still converging to the optimal action-value function $Q^*$~\cite{sutton}. On the other hand, \emph{on-policy} methods, such as the later discussed PPO, learn the value of the exact policy the agent is currently executing.

The TD error and Q-learning update rule form the foundation used in virtually every modern algorithm discussed in this thesis. However, these tabular methods cannot scale to environments with large or continuous state spaces, which motivates the introduction of function approximation in the following chapter.

\chapter{Deep Reinforcement Learning}
\label{chap:deep_rl}

Classical reinforcement learning often represents value functions in tabular form, but as state and action spaces grow from discrete settings to high-dimensional continuous domains, storing all value estimates in lookup tables becomes computationally impossible~\cite{sutton}. The solution is to approximate value functions using parametric models, and neural networks have become the standard \emph{function approximators} for these larger problems~\cite{spinningup2018}.
Formally, instead of storing $V^{\pi}(s)$ for every state $s$, we introduce an approximate value function $\hat V(s, w)$ with a weight vector $w \in \mathbb{R}^d$ so that $\hat{V}(s, w) \approx V^\pi(s)$~\cite{sutton}. Since the number of parameters $d$ is typically much lower than the number of possible states, updating $\hat{V}$ for one state also indirectly changes the value estimates for many similar states; this is a form of \emph{generalization}~\cite{sutton}.

\section{Neural Networks as Function Approximators}
A deep neural network can approximate value functions or policies across vast state spaces without explicitly storing each state-action pair~\cite{mnih2015human}. Apart from scaling to very high-dimensional inputs, neural networks in particular are great at representing highly non-linear relationships between states and values or policies~\cite{spinningup2018}. They also naturally support feature learning, where networks learn intermediate representations that are useful for predicting returns or action advantages~\cite{mnih2015human}. 
In multi-agent environments with continuous and combinatorial observations, deep neural networks are necessary because the joint state of all agents cannot feasibly be represented in a tabular value function.

\paragraph{Deep Q-Network (DQN)}\mbox{}\\
A big breakthrough was the example of Deep Q-Network (DQN), which showed that a convolutional neural network can approximate the action-value function $Q(s,a)$ directly from raw Atari game pixels and achieve human-level performance across many games~\cite{mnih2015human}. The network takes a stack of 4 recent grayscale frames as input and outputs estimated Q-value for each possible action, while trying to minimize the temporal difference target~\cite{mnih2015human}. 
DQN stabilizes learning with two techniques: \emph{experience replay}, which randomly samples past transitions to break correlation in the observation sequence, and a separate \emph{target network}, which periodically freezes Q-value estimates to provide a stable learning target~\cite{mnih2015human}. This is the core update rule:
\[
  \mathbf{w}_{t+1} = \mathbf{w}_t + \alpha \left( R_t + \gamma \max_{a'} \hat{Q}(S_{t+1}, a'; \mathbf{w}^-) - \hat{Q}(S_t, A_t; \mathbf{w}_t) \right) \nabla_\mathbf{w} \hat{Q}(S_t, A_t; \mathbf{w}_t)
\]
where $\alpha$ is the learning rate and the network is updated using stochastic gradient descent~\cite{mnih2015human}. A simplified DQN architecture is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:nn-qlearning}. DQN demonstrated the viability of combining deep learning with RL and inspired many subsequent advances in both value-based and policy-based methods.

\section{Policy Gradient Methods}
Instead of learning an action-value function $\hat Q(s,a)$ like DQN, these methods learn the \emph{policy directly} as $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ and optimize expected return $J(\theta)$ by gradient ascent on the policy parameters~\cite{sutton}.

Because it is not possible to directly backpropagate through the environment's state transitions due to unknown dynamics, the gradient of the performance must be reformulated~\cite{spinningup2018}. By applying the Policy Gradient Theorem, the gradient can be computed purely from sampled trajectories without requiring the derivative of the environment's transition probabilities~\cite{sutton}:
\[
\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T} \underbrace{\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t)}_{\text{push direction}}\, \underbrace{Q^{\pi_\theta}(S_t,A_t)}_{\text{scale by return}} \right].
\]
Actions with high returns are reinforced; those with low returns are suppressed~\cite{spinningup2018}.

\paragraph{REINFORCE and Advantage}\mbox{}\\
The foundational policy gradient algorithm is REINFORCE, which uses the actual observed episodic returns (Monte Carlo style) to estimate the true action-value function~\cite{sutton}. The agent plays out a trajectory and then increases the log-probability of actions proportional to the total return earned~\cite{sutton}. While conceptually simple and unbiased, relying on full episodic returns causes severe variance, leading to slow and unstable learning~\cite{sutton}.

To stabilize training, a standard improvement is to subtract a baseline value function $V^{\pi_\theta}(S_t)$ from the return, arriving at the \emph{advantage function}~\cite{sutton}:
\[
A^{\pi_\theta}(S_t,A_t) = \underbrace{Q^{\pi_\theta}(S_t,A_t)}_{\text{this action}} - \underbrace{V^{\pi_\theta}(S_t)}_{\text{state average}},
\]
This makes gradient updates focus on relative improvements and is the standard for data efficiency and stability in modern RL algorithms~\cite{sutton, spinningup2018}. Figure~\ref{fig:policy-update} illustrates this more intuitively.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{obrazky-figures/policy_update.drawio.pdf}
    \caption[Policy Gradient Update]{Policy gradient update over the six KAZ actions. Actions with positive advantage $A^{\pi_\theta} > 0$ (green) gain probability mass; those with negative advantage (red) lose it.}
    \label{fig:policy-update}
\end{figure}

However, how do we obtain $V^{\pi_\theta}(S_t)$ to compute the advantage, and must we still wait for full episode rollouts like in REINFORCE~\cite{sutton}? These questions are addressed by actor-critic methods.


\section{Actor-Critic Methods}
\emph{Actor-critic methods} sit between pure value-based methods and pure policy-gradient methods by maintaining two separate function approximators: a \emph{policy} (actor) and a \emph{value function} (critic)~\cite{sutton}.

\paragraph{Architecture and Roles}\mbox{}\\
The actor is a parametrized policy $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ that maps states to a distribution over actions and is the component responsible for making decisions~\cite{sutton}. The critic is a parametrized value function, usually a state-value $V_\phi(s)$ or an action-value $Q_\phi(s,a)$, that predicts the expected return when following the current policy~\cite{sutton, zhang2021}. Basically, the actor \emph{proposes} behavior and the critic \emph{judges} it by summarizing the performance of the policy into a scalar value~\cite{zhang2021}. This architecture differs from pure value-based methods, where the policy is implicitly `greedy' with respect to a learned Q-function~\cite{sutton}. In actor-critic frameworks, the policy and value function are conceptually independent of each other and are represented by two distinct neural networks. However, in deep RL practice, they generally share a common feature extractor with separate output heads for the policy and value~\cite{spinningup2018}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.7\textwidth]{obrazky-figures/actor-critic2.drawio.pdf}
    \caption[Actor-Critic Architecture]{Actor-critic architecture. The actor $\pi_\theta$ selects actions from state $S_t$; the critic $V_\phi$ evaluates them by computing the advantage $\hat{A}_t$ from the environment reward, which is fed back to guide the actor's updates.}
    \label{fig:actor-critic}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Learning Signal}\mbox{}\\
The actor uses the TD error $\delta_t$ directly as a learning signal: if $\delta_t > 0$, the taken action was better than expected, so the actor increases the probability of similar actions; if $\delta_t < 0$, it decreases it~\cite{sutton}. This way, the critic effectively provides an online advantage estimate without waiting for full episode returns, which leads to faster and more stable learning overall. However, this could introduce bias if the critic's signal is imperfect~\cite{konda2000, spinningup2018}. Most modern deep RL algorithms share this actor-critic backbone, although with different architectural additions and design choices~\cite{spinningup2018}.

\section{Proximal Policy Optimization}
\label{sec:ppo}
The algorithm chosen for this environment and thesis scenario is MAPPO, which is a multi-agent extension of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)~\cite{yu2021mappo}. Before adding ideas and extensions from the multi-agent layer, it is helpful to first thoroughly explain its fundamental algorithm. PPO builds on the actor-critic framework~\cite{konda2000} but with an innovation, which is preventing policy from changing \emph{too drastically} in a single update, leading to significantly more stable training~\cite{schulman2017ppo}.

\subsection{The Policy Update Problem}
Imagine an agent that has been exploring a maze and has finally learned a decent path to the exit. Now the standard policy gradient update comes, and it happens to be too large, and completely diverges the agent's policy, making it run into walls again. This is a problem because our collected data were generated under the old policy, so large policy changes make the updates unreliable and can cause sudden training collapse. PPO solves this with a simple clipping mechanism~\cite{schulman2017ppo}.

\paragraph{The Probability Ratio and Clipping}\mbox{}\\
To take clipping into effect, we first determine \emph{how much} the policy has changed. PPO does this through the \emph{probability ratio}, which compares the probability of taking the same action under the new policy versus the old one:
\[
r_t(\theta)
= \frac{\pi_\theta(A_t \mid S_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(A_t \mid S_t)}.
\]
When $r_t(\theta) = 1$, the policies are identical for action $A_t$; values above or below $1$ indicate the new policy makes $A_t$ more or less likely. Then, PPO takes this ratio and clips it to stay within $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$ (typically $\epsilon \approx 0.2$), so once the policy has changed noticeably, further updates in that direction do not improve the objective. The clipped surrogate is
\[
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_t\!\left[
\min\!\left(
r_t(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t,\;
\text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\,\hat{A}_t
\right)\right],
\]
where $\hat{A}_t$ is the advantage estimate and the \emph{min} ensures conservative updates~\cite{schulman2017ppo}. This effect is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ppo-clip}. Because clipping constrains how much the policy can change, the same rollout can be safely reused for multiple epochs of minibatch gradient descent without the updates becoming too stale or destructive, making PPO significantly more sample-efficient than classic policy gradient methods~\cite{spinningup2018}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.9\textwidth]{obrazky-figures/ppo_clip.pdf}
    \caption[PPO Clipping]{PPO clipping for positive advantage $\hat{A}>0$ (left, green) and negative advantage $\hat{A}<0$ (right, red). The objective flattens only at $r=1+\epsilon$ for $\hat{A}>0$ and at $r=1-\epsilon$ for $\hat{A}<0$.}
    \label{fig:ppo-clip}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Generalized Advantage Estimation}
To compute the advantage $\hat{A}_t$, PPO uses Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE), that finds a middle ground by blending TD errors across multiple time steps with an exponential decay~\cite{schulman2016gae}:
\[
\hat{A}_t
= \sum_{l=0}^{T-1-t} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l},
\]
where $\delta_t = R_t + \gamma V_\phi(S_{t+1}) - V_\phi(S_t)$ is the TD error, $\lambda \in [0,1]$ balances bias and variance; when $\lambda=1$ it becomes the full Monte Carlo return (in practice, $\lambda \approx 0.95$ is used)~\cite{schulman2016gae}. The critic is then trained to minimize the squared error between predictions $V_\phi(S_t)$ and the target returns $\hat{R}_t = \hat{A}_t + V_{\phi_{\mathrm{old}}}(S_t)$~\cite{schulman2017ppo}. MAPPO directly builds on this foundation by extending it to the multi-agent setting.


\chapter{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning}
\label{chap:marl}
Multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) extends RL to settings where multiple agents operate in a shared environment (Figure~\ref{fig:marl-loop}). This raises problems that barely exist in standard RL: the environment becomes non-stationary from each agent's perspective as others update their policies concurrently, and credit assignment across agents grows difficult~\cite{albrecht2024marl, zhang2021}. But MARL also opens up genuinely interesting phenomena -- how agents communicate, how \emph{coordinated strategies can emerge} from simple reward signals. In a football game, explicit communication between players is essential for optimal play, but what happens when the players are RL agents with only observations and reward feedback? Before addressing such questions, it is useful to see how multi-agent environments are defined in game-theoretic terms.

\section{Types of Games}
A multi-agent environment has many connections to game theory, especially in how these environments are formally modeled as types of games~\cite{albrecht2024marl}. At the one-step level, these reduce to the simplest normal-form (matrix) games. For environments that have sequential interaction, these are known as stochastic or Markov games. Markov games are a multi-agent generalization of Markov decision processes where several players choose actions, receive (possibly different) rewards, and jointly influence the state transition dynamics~\cite{littman1994markov, albrecht2024marl}.

An important distinction is between different payoff structures. Games like chess are competitive \emph{zero-sum games} because from one player's perspective you either get +1 reward for a win or -1 reward for a loss, therefore the sum of possible rewards is 0. In a cooperative environment where all share the same reward, this is known as a \emph{common reward game}~\cite{albrecht2024marl}. There are also \emph{general-sum games}, where agents have individual reward functions that are neither strictly opposed nor perfectly aligned~\cite{albrecht2024marl}.
But sometimes, the agent cannot see the entire playing field or exactly every player at every step. This is known as \emph{partial observability} and leads to \emph{partially observable stochastic games} (POSGs) and, in fully cooperative common reward games, to \emph{decentralized partially observable MDPs} (Dec-POMDPs), which are the usual formal model for cooperative MARL tasks~\cite{oliehoek2016concise, albrecht2024marl}. Figure~\ref{fig:game-models} summarizes the relationship between these game models.

Dec-POMDP is another special case with common reward payoff structure and decentralized observation policies, and is the natural starting formal model for the original KAZ mechanics when all agents share a team reward.}

In this thesis, the transition dynamics, observations, and termination conditions stay fixed across experiments, but the reward function is varied. Formally, this means the underlying game mechanics stay the same while the payoff structure changes: some schemes remain strongly cooperative, while others introduce more individualized or mixed-motive incentives. So Dec-POMDP is the right starting model for the base task, but not every reward scheme used later stays common-reward in the strict sense.


\section{The Multi-Agent Problem}
In single-agent RL, the environment is usually modeled as a stationary MDP. In MARL, this assumption breaks down because other learning agents are part of the environment, so transition and reward distributions shift as agents update their policies concurrently. This \emph{non-stationarity} makes even simple independent algorithms unstable, especially in general-sum settings where agents' objectives are not aligned~\cite{albrecht2024marl, zhang2021}.

The second issue is scalability. With 2 agents each having 6 actions, the joint action space is $6^2 = 36$; with 4 agents it grows to $6^4 = 1{,}296$. This interlocks with the \emph{multi-agent credit assignment problem}: how to determine which agents and actions were responsible for a change in returns when a single global reward provides sparse and entangled feedback~\cite{albrecht2024marl}. Partial observability compounds both problems. When agents can only access local information, finding exact equilibria is often computationally intractable, and agents struggle to distinguish environmental noise from the evolving strategies of teammates~\cite{albrecht2024marl}. Figure~\ref{fig:marl-problems-1} illustrates three problems mentioned.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{obrazky-figures/marl_problems1.pdf}
    \caption[Multi-agent problems 1]{Three MARL problems. Left: \emph{partial observability} -- agent $A$ targets a zombie in its vision radius while a boss outside escapes undetected. Centre: \emph{non-stationarity} -- $A$'s optimal $Q^A(s,a)$ shifts each time $B$'s policy updates. Right: \emph{credit assignment} -- four agents produce a shared reward $R_t$ but no signal indicates which action caused it.}
    \label{fig:marl-problems-1}
\end{figure}


Finally, MARL often involves social dilemma settings where agents face competitive tendencies to maximize individual reward rather than working toward the overall success of the group. This leads to agents getting trapped in inefficient outcomes even when mutual coordination would pay off. Unlike the classic Prisoner's Dilemma, these are not one-off choices but here agents are making continuous, complex decisions over long period of time.~\cite{leibo2017ssd}.

Avoiding these problems requires learning mechanisms that go beyond simple, short-term reward maximization~\cite{albrecht2024marl}.

\section{Solution Concepts and Algorithms}
Now considering all these problems defined, this section describes how agents should be trained and deployed. Modern cooperative MARL approaches consist of three training schemes: \emph{centralized training and execution} (CTE), \emph{decentralized training and execution} (DTDE), and \emph{centralized training with decentralized execution} (CTDE)~\cite{albrecht2024marl}. 

\subsection{Centralized Training with Decentralized Execution (CTDE)}
\label{ctde}

CTDE has become the dominant paradigm in modern MARL. During training, agents are optimized using centralized or ``privileged'' information, but at deployment each agent acts using only its local observation. This split addresses non-stationarity and credit assignment while preserving decentralized control at test time~\cite{albrecht2024marl}.

CTDE methods fall into two families. \emph{Value-decomposition} methods such as Value-Decomposition Networks (VDN) or Q-value Mixing Networks (QMIX) learn per-agent utilities combined by a centralized mixing function to approximate a joint action-value function~\cite{sunehag2018vdn,rashid2018qmix}.
\emph{Centralized-critic} methods such as Multi-Agent Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (MADDPG),
Counterfactual Multi-Agent Policy Gradients (COMA), or Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO)
instead give each decentralized policy a a critic with access to global information, such as the full state and, in some algorithms, joint actions during
training~\cite{lowe2017maddpg,foerster2018coma,yu2021mappo}.

\section{Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization}
Multi-agent proximal policy optimization (MAPPO) is a fairly straightforward multi-agent extension of PPO with a CTDE architecture. Compared to other centralized-critic algorithms, such as COMA~\cite{foerster2018coma}, MAPPO is architecturally simpler, but still achieves state-of-the-art performance on cooperative benchmarks such as StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge, Google Research Football, and Hanabi~\cite{yu2021mappo}, which makes it a clean testing ground for analyzing emergent behavior.


\subsection{Distinctions from Single-Agent PPO}
Structurally, there are three main differences from single-agent PPO. First, instead of collecting experience from one agent, we collect from all $n$ agents simultaneously. At each time step $t$, every agent $i$ receives its own local observation $o_t^i$, picks an action $A_t^i$, and the environment emits reward signal(s)~\cite{oliehoek2016concise, albrecht2024marl}. Second is the CTDE principle; the centralized critic $V_\phi(S_t)$ is given the full state of the environment during training, while each actor (policy) only has access to its own local observation $o_t^i$~\cite{foerster2018coma, albrecht2024marl}. And third, the PPO clipped objective and critic loss are computed per agent and then averaged across all agents and time steps~\cite{yu2021mappo}. The clipping mechanism itself, GAE, and minibatch reuse are otherwise relatively the same. 

\paragraph{Formal Setup}\mbox{}\\
MAPPO maintains a shared decentralized policy $\pi_\theta(A_t^i \mid o_t^i, h_t^i)$ for all agents $i$, with parameters $\theta$ and optional recurrent hidden state $h_t^i$ to address partial observability~\cite{yu2021mappo}. It also learns a centralized value function $V_\phi(S_t)$, or more generally $V_\phi(\tilde S_t)$ when training has access to global information $\tilde S_t$, with parameters $\phi$~\cite{yu2021mappo}. In the standard cooperative setting, the objective is to learn a decentralized joint policy $\pi_\theta = (\pi_\theta^1,\dots,\pi_\theta^n)$ that maximizes the expected discounted return
\[
J(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sum_{t=0}^{T} \gamma^t R_t \Bigr],
\]
where execution remains decentralized and each agent acts only from its own local information~\cite{yu2021mappo}. This corresponds to the usual Dec-POMDP/common-reward formulation.

\subsection{MAPPO Objective}

MAPPO extends the PPO clipped surrogate objective for multiple agents by aggregating the per-agent PPO losses over all agents and time steps~\cite{yu2021mappo, schulman2017ppo}. To track how much the policy has changed for each specific agent $i$, given a trajectory collected under the old policy $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$, the per-agent probability ratio is~\cite{yu2021mappo, schulman2017ppo}:
\[
r_t^i(\theta)
= \frac{\pi_\theta(A_t^i \mid o_t^i, h_t^i)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(A_t^i \mid o_t^i, h_t^i)}.
\]
Typically in environments with shared reward and a single centralized value function, agents share the same advantage estimate $\hat{A}_t$, computed via GAE from the \emph{centralized} critic as described in Section~\ref{sec:ppo}, the only difference being that the critic now operates on the global state $S_t$ instead of a single agent's observation. The clipped policy loss then simply averages the per-agent PPO objectives~\cite{yu2021mappo, schulman2017ppo}:
\[
L^{\text{MAPPO}}_{\pi}(\theta)
= \mathbb{E}_{t,i}\Bigl[
\min\bigl(
r_t^i(\theta)\,\hat{A}_t,\;
\operatorname{clip}(r_t^i(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\,\hat{A}_t
\bigr)
\Bigr],
\]
and the critic loss remains the same squared error between predicted and target returns~\cite{yu2021mappo}:
\[
L^{\text{MAPPO}}_{V}(\phi)
= \mathbb{E}_t\Bigl[(V_\phi(S_t) - \hat{R}_t)^2\Bigr].
\]
The final combined loss is:
\[
\mathcal{L}(\theta,\phi)
= \underbrace{- L^{\text{MAPPO}}_{\pi}(\theta)}_{\text{policy gain}}
+ \underbrace{c_v L^{\text{MAPPO}}_{V}(\phi)}_{\text{critic error}}
- \underbrace{c_e H[\pi_\theta]}_{\text{entropy bonus}},
\]
where $c_v$ weights the critic loss and $c_e$ weights the entropy bonus, which keeps the policy stochastic by stopping it from prematurely converging to a single action~\cite{yu2021mappo}.

Intuitively, the centralized critic $V_\phi(S_t)$ learns to predict the \emph{joint} return of the whole team, while each decentralized actor update pushes the shared policy $\pi_\theta$ towards increasing team advantage, but only as long as the probability ratio $r_t^i(\theta)$ stays within the PPO clipping range. This preserves the stability benefits of PPO while exploiting extra information during training for faster and more stable learning in multi-agent tasks~\cite{yu2021mappo}. The more concrete data flow is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:mappo-schematic}.

The GAE block combines critic values and rewards into $\hat{A}_t$ and $\hat{R}_t$. \subsection{MAPPO Algorithm}
Algorithm~\ref{alg:mappo} shows simplified logic of the MAPPO training loop. It follows the standard PPO structure but collects data from all agents simultaneously before updating the shared networks.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
  \caption{Multi-Agent Proximal Policy Optimization (MAPPO), adapted from~\cite{yu2021mappo, schulman2017ppo, schulman2016gae}.}
  \label{alg:mappo}
  \begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \STATE \textbf{Input:} actor policy parameters $\theta_0$, value function $\phi_0$, $n$ agents
    \STATE \textbf{Hyperparameters:} clip $\epsilon$, discount $\gamma$, GAE $\lambda$, rollout $N$, epochs $K$, minibatch $M$
    \FOR{iteration $k = 0,1,2,\dots$}
      \STATE $\theta_{\text{old}} \leftarrow \theta_k$
      \STATE \textbf{// Collect rollout from all agents}
      \FOR{$t = 0$ to $N-1$}
        \FOR{each agent $i = 1,\dots,n$}
          \STATE Sample $A_t^i \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid o_t^i)$ \hfill \textit{(decentralized: local obs only)}
        \ENDFOR
        \STATE Execute joint action $\mathbf{A}_t$, observe team reward $R_t$, global state $S_{t+1}$, local obs $\{o_{t+1}^i\}$
      \ENDFOR
      \STATE \textbf{// Compute GAE advantages using centralized critic}
      \STATE Compute $\delta_t = R_t + \gamma V_\phi(S_{t+1}) - V_\phi(S_t)$ for all $t$ \hfill \textit{(centralized: global state)}
      \STATE Compute $\hat{A}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{T-1-t} (\gamma\lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}$ for all $t$
      \STATE Set target returns $\hat{R}_t \leftarrow \hat{A}_t + V_{\phi_{\mathrm{old}}}(S_t)$ and normalize advantages
      \STATE \textbf{// Update shared networks}
      \FOR{epoch $e = 1$ to $K$}
        \FOR{each minibatch $\mathcal{B}$ of size $M$}
          \STATE Compute per-agent ratio $r_t^i(\theta) = \pi_\theta(A_t^i \mid o_t^i) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(A_t^i \mid o_t^i)$
          \STATE $L_\pi = \mathbb{E}_{(t,i) \in \mathcal{B}}[\min(r_t^i \hat{A}_t, \operatorname{clip}(r_t^i, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \hat{A}_t)]$
          \STATE $L_V = \mathbb{E}_{t \in \mathcal{B}}[(V_\phi(S_t) - \hat{R}_t)^2]$
          \STATE Update $(\theta, \phi)$ via gradient descent on $-L_\pi + c_v L_V - c_e H[\pi_\theta]$
        \ENDFOR
      \ENDFOR
    \ENDFOR
  \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Parameter Sharing}
\label{subsec:param-sharing}
Without parameter sharing, $n$ agents may require $n$ separate policy networks, which would give each agent full independence, but would be expensive to train. To address the scalability problem, it is often better to employ \emph{parameter sharing} in the actor structure, especially if agents share the same action space and play the same role~\cite{albrecht2024marl}. All agents are controlled by a single shared policy network, but each agent still develops diverse behavior because they receive different observations, ending up in different situations during play. The number of trainable parameters therefore stays constant regardless of team size, and every agent's 
experience contributes to training the same network~\cite{yu2021mappo}. This works well for homogeneous teams, but in heterogeneous settings, where different sets of agents have different jobs, full sharing may suppress this role-specific specialization. \emph{Type-based sharing} addresses this by maintaining one network per agent type. This thesis experiments with all three variants (Figure~\ref{fig:parameter-sharing}) to analyze how this choice influences emergent coordination regimes and role structure~\cite{albrecht2024marl}.

\section{Emergent Coordination, Roles, and Communication}
After establishing the fundamental concepts of MARL, it is time to focus on what makes multi-agent RL truly interesting: the situations where agents start to coordinate, divide roles, or develop stable conventions without these patterns being scripted in advance. In small heterogeneous teams, emergence often appears less like randomly forming subgroups and more like developing stable roles where agents actively rely on each other. These phenomena are \emph{emergent} in the sense that they arise from the interaction of learning dynamics, reward functions, and environment mechanics rather than from hard-coded tactical rules.

\subsection{Emergent Coordination}
\emph{Emergent coordination} means that when agents learn simultaneously, their joint behavior starts to reveal a certain global structure that cannot be understood by just looking at any single isolated agent. For example, when agents learn to cover different parts of the map, or to take complementary roles in an attack-defense pattern, or to time their actions so that they help each other instead of getting in each other's way. 
In cooperative MARL benchmarks like SMAC, strong policies almost always display this behavior with respect to scalar reward signals~\cite{yu2021mappo,rashid2018qmix}. In this thesis, we design situations where mutual support and shared risk could benefit the team, and then empirically test whether agents actually learn to exploit these opportunities and whether doing so turns out to be optimal.

\subsection{Reward Design for Coordination Regimes}
\label{subsec:reward}
Emergent behavior is heavily shaped by reward function design. Leibo et al.\ demonstrated this in simple grid-world harvesting games, where a small change to the reward structure was enough to flip the system from peaceful cooperation to aggressive overexploitation~\cite{leibo2017ssd}. Counter-intuitively, in resource-scarce environments, agents who care more about the future are actually more likely to learn aggressive policies because they recognize the long-term payoff of eliminating competition~\cite{leibo2017ssd}. Network capacity also matters: complex behaviors only emerge if the agent has sufficient representational capacity to execute them~\cite{leibo2017ssd}. In mixed-motive settings, short-term selfish behavior such as free riding can be tempting even when long-term group welfare would be higher under cooperation, so naive reward design alone is rarely sufficient~\cite{leibo2017ssd, albrecht2024marl}.
In this thesis, reward engineering is not treated as a source of sociality by itself. KAZ already contains hard interdependence through asymmetric roles and shared failure conditions. Reward design is instead used as an experimental variable to determine which coordination regime becomes stable under the same base task.

\subsection{Emergent Communication}
Another interesting emergent behavior is how agents communicate with each other and what drives them to do so. In many MARL instances, agents are given \emph{differentiable communication channels}, where agents send discrete or continuous messages, which are added to their observation or policy networks, and eventually learn an artificial ``language'' specialized for the task~\cite{lazaridou2020emergent}. 
Though, measuring whether such communication is truly useful is tricky. Lowe et al.\ showed that naive metrics like mutual information between messages and actions can be misleading, since agents might send messages that correlate with their behavior but have no real effect on teammates~\cite{lowe2019pitfalls}. The recommended solution here is to compare tasks with and without channels or to scramble the channel and verify if performance differs. This thesis doesn't explore explicit channels; instead, it looks for communication-like coordination through behavioral metrics.

\subsection{Coalitions, Roles, and Social Structure}
Multi-agent systems can develop complex social structures beyond simple collective cooperation. Rachum et al.\ showed that agents in small environments can spontaneously form \emph{dominance hierarchies} as a tool for conflict resolution~\cite{rachum2024dominance}. Such structural differences can be quantified by frameworks like Role Diversity, which measure differences between actions, trajectories, and value contributions~\cite{hu2022policy}. Importantly, these differences interact with architectural choices: environments with high action-based diversity can degrade under full parameter sharing, while settings with high trajectory divergence may benefit less from explicit communication~\cite{hu2022policy}.

\chapter{Chosen Environment}
\label{chap:environment}

Picking the right environment matters. Since the primary goal is studying reward-sensitive coordination in a heterogeneous team, the chosen environment needs asymmetric roles, potential for partial credit assignment, and room for spatial coordination.
The initial plan was to build a custom environment from scratch: a 10$\times$10 grid with resources, enemies, and a boss. In practice, agents failed to learn even basic tasks, so coordination analysis on top of that was pointless. This led to KAZ.

\section{Knights Archers Zombies}
Knights Archers Zombies (KAZ) is a cooperative 2D game where knights and archers defend the bottom of the screen from zombies descending from the top. It is part of the PettingZoo ``butterfly'' environments and is commonly used as a benchmark in MARL libraries.

\paragraph{Original Knights Archers Zombies}\mbox{}\\
Agents are separated into two roles -- knights and archers -- and share a set of six discrete actions: \textsc{noop}, \textsc{forward}, \textsc{backward}, \textsc{rotate\_left}, \textsc{rotate\_right}, and \textsc{attack}. The attack action is distinct for each role: knights swing a sword (flail), and archers shoot long-range arrows. The goal is to survive as long as possible by killing zombies that spawn at fixed intervals at a random horizontal position along the top of the screen and descend with a zig-zag movement. Although the zombies do not actively chase agents, they remain dangerous because contact with them causes damage and their lateral motion makes their trajectories less predictable. The episode ends when a zombie reaches the bottom of the screen or when the 900-step limit is reached.
The two distinct roles make the agents heterogeneous. Because zombie spawn positions and lateral movement vary with the random seed, dealing with them requires cooperation. The screen is large enough for agents to move freely, which makes spatial behavior easy to observe.
This raises several questions worth investigating, such as what coordination regimes emerge, how much reward design actually matters, and which environment mechanics dominate the resulting team structure.

\subsection{Custom KAZ Implementation}
While the environment is promising, the first training runs exposed two problems. First, archers can spam arrows from a distance while knights struggle to get close without dying. This made archers overpowered -- they ended up stealing most kills, leaving the environment too imbalanced for meaningful cooperation analysis. Second, there is no rising difficulty, so agents could settle on a local optimum and survive indefinitely until the step limit. To get a fair but not stale baseline for cooperation analysis, we customized the environment with the following additions, visually summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:kaz-entities-2}.

\paragraph{Boss enemy and wave system}\mbox{}\\
Bosses add a second enemy type with different dynamics. Like regular zombies, they spawn at fixed intervals (every 200~steps) at a random horizontal position. They have more hit points (HP), slightly different movement, and their attack instantly kills any agent close by. Because bosses take considerably more effort to bring down, agents need to commit attention to them. Killing a boss advances the wave counter, which increases both zombie and boss HP. Waves act as a difficulty scaler that prevents agents from settling into a static strategy, and the wave count itself serves as a rough measure of team progress.

\paragraph{Leveling / XP system}\mbox{}\\
During testing, the wave system overwhelmed agents too quickly because their damage output stayed flat while enemy HP grew. To address this, a leveling system was added: agents receive experience points (XP) for each kill (amounts differ by enemy type), and after accumulating enough XP they level up, gaining more base HP and attack damage (ATK). Beyond offsetting the rising difficulty, leveling creates asymmetric agent states within an episode, which makes kill distribution matter more: An agent that falls behind in XP becomes weaker relative to the wave, so level balance across the team becomes a real concern and a target for reward shaping.

\paragraph{Shielded Zombies}\mbox{}\\
Because archers still dominated the kill count, the last addition was shielded zombies. These spawn less frequently and carry a shield that only a knight can break, so archers cannot damage them until the shield is down. This forces a dependency on knights: if all knights die, shielded zombies become unstoppable and can walk through to the bottom. It also gives archers a reason to protect their knights rather than ignoring them, encouraging mutual protection. In later chapters, we isolate the effect of this mechanic by testing a variant in which archers can also break shields and receive the same shield-break reward.

\section{JAX Implementation}
The original KAZ environment is built on PyGame, which works well for playing games but is not suitable for large-scale MARL training, where each step goes through the Python interpreter sequentially, and GPU utilization is poor. The solution is to rebuild the environment entirely in JAX, which compiles and executes array operations directly on GPU hardware~\cite{jax2018}.

\paragraph{Functional Design and Immutable State}\mbox{}\\
JAX requires \emph{pure} functions with no side effects or mutable state~\cite{jax2018}. The custom KAZ environment is built around two pure functions: \texttt{reset(rng, config)} and \texttt{step(state, actions, config)}, neither of which modifies anything in place. The entire game state is packed into \texttt{KAZState}, a \texttt{NamedTuple} of JAX arrays that forms a valid \emph{pytree} for the compiler to traverse and transform~\cite{jax2018}. Because JAX requires fixed array shapes at compile time, the state always allocates slots for all possible entities, and the unused ones are simply masked out with a boolean flag.

\paragraph{JIT Compilation and Parallel Environments}\mbox{}\\
Expressing the environment as pure functions over pytree state unlocks two JAX primitives~\cite{jax2018}. \texttt{jax.jit} compiles a function on its first call via XLA (Accelerated Linear Algebra) and runs all subsequent calls as a native GPU kernel. \texttt{jax.vmap} vectorizes over a batch dimension, lifting \texttt{step} to operate on 256 independent environment states in a single GPU call.

\paragraph{Scan-Based Rollout}\mbox{}\\
Python \texttt{for} loops inside JIT-compiled code get statically unrolled into the computation graph. \texttt{jax.lax.scan} avoids this by expressing the rollout as a single compiled loop: it applies the environment step and action selection for $T$ steps, accumulating transitions into one stacked array. Combined with \texttt{vmap}, the full collection phase of each MAPPO update is one compiled rollout call operation~\cite{jax2018}. Figure~\ref{fig:jax-schematic} tries to clarify the abstract JAX-based functions.


\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{obrazky-figures/JAX.drawio.pdf}
    \caption[JAX compilation and scan-based rollout]{Each JAX layer adds a transformation -- \texttt{jax.jit} compiles the step into a native GPU kernel, \texttt{jax.vmap} lifts it over $N=256$ parallel environments, and \texttt{lax.scan} unrolls $T=128$ timesteps -- leading to the full rollout of $N \times T$ transitions in a single GPU call.}
    \label{fig:jax-schematic}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Observation Space}\mbox{}\\
From a single agent's perspective, the environment is not encoded as a grid or an image. Instead, we use a \emph{relative, entity-centric} representation where except for the agent's own row, all positions are given as coordinates relative to the observing agent, normalized by screen dimensions, with distances normalized by the screen diagonal. Each agent receives a 2D observation matrix shaped $(\text{num\_rows},\; 8)$, where every row represents a different entity in the environment. All entity types share the same schema:
  \[
    \bigl[\;
      \underbrace{d_\text{norm}}_{\text{distance}},\;
      \underbrace{\Delta x,\; \Delta y}_{\text{relative pos.}},\;
      \underbrace{\text{dir}_x,\; \text{dir}_y}_{\text{facing}},\;
      \underbrace{\text{hp\_ratio}}_{\text{health}},\;
      \underbrace{\text{extra}_1,\; \text{extra}_2}_{\text{type-specific}}
    \;\bigr],
  \]
where the two extra columns carry type-specific information such as level, shield, status, or type marker that distinguishes bosses from regular zombies.
Partial observability is controlled by a configurable \emph{vision radius} that zeroes out entities beyond this distance.
  
Consistent with the CTDE paradigm, the centralized critic's global world state is formed by concatenating the full observations of all agents into a single flat vector while each actor still operates only on its own local observation.

\paragraph{Neural Network Stack}\mbox{}\\
Depending on the parameter-sharing setting, we use a shared/separate actor network that maps local observations to action logits, and a centralized critic that maps the global world state to a scalar value. Both are three-layer multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) with 256 hidden units and \texttt{tanh} activations. The implementation uses Flax Linen~\cite{flax2020} for the network definitions, Distrax~\cite{deepmind2020jax} for sampling from the categorical action distribution, and Optax~\cite{deepmind2020jax} for Adam with gradient clipping. All parameters, optimizer state, and random keys are bundled into a single \texttt{TrainState} pytree, which is the only object carried forward across update iterations.

\section{Reward Design}
\label{sec:reward_design}
As discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:reward}, reward shaping is the main experimental lever for encouraging or suppressing specific coordination patterns. To study its effect, seven distinct reward schemes were designed. The original KAZ environment used a simple $+1.0$ reward per zombie kill, which is a solid baseline for extensions. With the environment customization, the base rewards were extended to $+1.5$ per shielded zombie, $+0.75$ per shield break, and $+3.0$ per boss. All seven policies share the same transition dynamics, observation model, and episode rules; what changes is only the reward function. One-step visualizations of all policies are shown in Figures~\ref{fig:baseline-shared-egalitarian-survival} and \ref{fig:coalition-zero-sum-territorial}. Formally, the mechanics stay fixed while the payoff structure is varied. Importantly, the environment is already cooperation-biased through shield breaking and shared failure conditions, so these rewards should be read as steering the \emph{style} of coordination more than creating interdependence from nothing.

\paragraph{Baseline}\mbox{}\\
Only base kill rewards, without any additional shaping. Each agent is rewarded solely for its own kills. Any coordination that shows up under this policy must come from the environment structure rather than reward engineering.

\paragraph{Shared Reward}\mbox{}\\
Pools 50\% of all kill rewards into a team pot each step, split equally among agents who took the attack action. The remaining 50\% stays with the killer. So if an archer lands a kill, the knight swinging nearby also gets a cut.

\paragraph{Egalitarian}\mbox{}\\
Penalizes XP inequality. \emph{Gini penalty}: $-0.015 \times (G - 0.3)$ applied to all agents when the Gini coefficient exceeds $0.3$. \emph{Catch-up bonus}: $+1.5$ when the lowest-XP agent lands a kill.


\paragraph{Survival}\mbox{}\\
\emph{Alive reward}: $+0.025 \times (\text{alive count} / \text{total agents})$ per step per alive agent. \emph{Death penalty}: $-2.0$ to all surviving agents when a teammate dies.

    \centering

    \label{fig:baseline-shared-egalitarian-survival}
\end{figure}


\paragraph{Coalition}\mbox{}\\
When an agent kills an enemy, 70\% of the kill reward is shared equally among alive allies within 200\,px. The killer keeps full base reward. The 200\,px radius matches the clustering coefficient threshold used in Chapter~\ref{chap:metrics}. 

\paragraph{Zero-Sum}\mbox{}\\
The killer receives $+1.0 \times$ kill value as bonus while all other alive agents split a penalty of equal magnitude. The signal is individually competitive, where helping a teammate actively hurts you.

\paragraph{Territorial}\mbox{}\\
Screen split at $x = \text{width}/2$. Archers own the left half, knights the right. Kill bonus of $+0.5\times$ kill value in own zone; $-0.005$ per-step penalty outside. The idea is to see whether explicit spatial ownership pushes agents to divide the map rather than cluster together.

\chapter{Measuring Emergent Coalitions}
\label{chap:metrics}

How do we determine whether two policies survive for the same reason? It is easy to eyeball patterns that are not really there, and reward alone cannot answer this. A team of agents could achieve the same total return through completely different social structures. To distinguish between these outcomes, this chapter introduces a set of post-hoc metrics computed over evaluation trajectories after training, consistent with prior work on emergent coordination, role diversity, and behavioral heterogeneity in cooperative MARL~\cite{albrecht2024marl, hu2022policy, bettini2024snd}.

Twelve metrics form the core of the analysis, organized into multiple sections. No single metric proves coalition formation on its own -- a team could be clustered tightly yet never coordinate attacks, or survive well while depending entirely on one role, which is why the metrics are most informative when read \emph{together}. Appendix~\ref{sec:additional_metrics} collects simpler supplementary diagnostics such as archer attack frequency and idle ratio, which are occasionally used in the final interpretation.

Throughout this chapter, let $\mathbf{p}_i(t)=(x_i(t),y_i(t))$ denote the position of agent $i$ at time step $t$, let $\mathbf{e}_j(t)$ denote the position of enemy $j$, and let $T$ denote the episode length in timesteps.

\paragraph{Replay Browser}\mbox{}\\
In addition to quantitative metrics, a web-based replay browser was built (implemented in Svelte/SvelteKit) for more intuitive visual inspection of agent behavior. Users export episodes from selected checkpoints into compact replay files and load them into interactive playback with frame-by-frame navigation, per-step analytics, and toggleable metric overlays. Up to six replays can be loaded side-by-side, which makes comparing different reward schemes much easier. Playback supports single-frame stepping and four speeds (0.5$\times$ to 4$\times$), as well as an analytics tab (shown in the lower half of the UI in Figure~\ref{fig:replay-browser}) where step-wise metrics from all loaded replays are displayed in line graphs and comparison tables. To simplify access, the replay browser also includes a hosted replay catalog, so users can browse shared episodes without installing the training pipeline locally. The replay browser was especially useful for debugging gameplay and checking whether custom metrics matched observed behavior. Appendix~\ref{appendix:replay-browser} briefly documents the tool and gives the hosted deployment link.

\section{Spatial Structure and Cohesion}
The most immediate thing about a team is how its members are positioned. Knights need to be close because of their melee attacks; archers can stay back because they shoot from range. If agents learn role-appropriate positioning, their spatial arrangement may be a useful indicator of effective coordination. Prior work on multi-robot formation control~\cite{balch1998formation} has analyzed team behavior through clustering and relative positioning, and it was one of the inspirations for the following metrics.

\subsection{Clustering Coefficient}
The starting point is mean pairwise inter-agent distance, but that number is sensitive to outliers, where a single agent far from the group inflates the average even when the rest form a tight cluster, so a threshold-based metric handles this better. The clustering coefficient is the fraction of alive pairs within a fixed radius $r_c$:
\[
C_t = \frac{1}{|P_t|}\sum_{(i,j)\in P_t}
\mathbf{1}\left\{\|\mathbf{p}_i(t)-\mathbf{p}_j(t)\|_2 < r_c\right\},
\qquad
\bar C = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} C_t,
\]
where $P_t=\{(i,j): i<j,\ i \text{ and } j \text{ are alive at time } t\}$. The threshold $r_c = 200$ pixels matches the coalition reward sharing radius, allowing direct comparison of spatial compactness with incentive range. $C_t \approx 1$ means nearly every agent pair is within $200$ pixels; $C_t \approx 0$ means otherwise.

\subsection{Formation Score}

Proximity alone is not enough; an effective KAZ team should also show role-aware positioning. Intuitively, knights should be in front to absorb damage and break shields, and archers behind to deal sustained ranged damage. To measure this, let
\[
\bar y_A(t)=\frac{1}{|A_t|}\sum_{i\in A_t} y_i(t), \qquad
\bar y_K(t)=\frac{1}{|K_t|}\sum_{j\in K_t} y_j(t),
\]
where $A_t$ and $K_t$ are the sets of archers and knights alive at time step $t$. Since enemies descend from the top of the screen, lower $y$-values correspond to positions closer to the front line. The formation score is then
\[
F_t = \mathrm{clip}\!\left(0.5 + \frac{\bar y_A(t)-\bar y_K(t)}{H}, 0, 1\right),
\qquad
\bar F = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} F_t,
\]
where $H=720$ is the screen height in pixels, used to normalize the vertical difference. A value above 0.5 means knights are, on average, positioned in front of archers.

\subsection{Role Separation}
Role separation measures how far apart the two role groups are overall by the Euclidean distance between the centroids of alive archers and alive knights:
\[
\mathbf{c}_A(t)=\frac{1}{|A_t|}\sum_{i\in A_t}\mathbf{p}_i(t), \qquad
\mathbf{c}_K(t)=\frac{1}{|K_t|}\sum_{j\in K_t}\mathbf{p}_j(t),
\]
\[
R_t = \|\mathbf{c}_A(t)-\mathbf{c}_K(t)\|_2,
\qquad
\bar R = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} R_t.
\]
High role separation with high formation score indicates spatially distinct sub-teams with a clear front-back structure. Low role separation with high clustering suggests the team moves as a single mixed group.

\subsection{Coalition Lifetime Median}
Since spatial snapshots only tell us who is near whom at a given moment, it is useful to track how long pairs of agents stay close together over time. Two agents are considered close at timestep $t$ if both are alive and within radius $r = 350$px. For each pair, we record each continuous time interval during which they remain close. If $R_r$ is the set of all such intervals and $\ell_k$ is the length of interval $k$, then
\[
\tilde \ell_{350} = \mathrm{median}\{\ell_k\}_{k \in R_{350}}.
\]
High values mean agents tend to stay close for longer. Low values mean they only come close briefly. We use $r = 350$\,px instead of 200\,px to make the metric more relaxed and not limited to only very close pairs.

\section{Coordination, Differentiation, and Engagement}
\label{sec:coordination}

Spatial proximity is a prerequisite for coordination, but agents can be clustered in the same area while each attacks a different enemy. Measuring coordinated target selection is useful as a descriptive indicator, although higher target concentration does not necessarily imply better teamwork, as later analysis shows.

\subsection{Focus Fire Score}

The focus fire score measures whether multiple agents attack the same enemy at the same timestep. We geometrically resolve each agent's attack: an archer is treated as targeting an enemy if the enemy is within $400$ pixels and falls inside a roughly 60-degree forward cone, and a knight targets an enemy if it is within its 70-pixel sword arc radius.

Given the set of attacking agents $T_t$ at timestep $t$, we define $\mathrm{coord}_i(t) = 1$ if agent $i$ attacks an enemy that is simultaneously targeted by at least one teammate, and 0 otherwise. The focus fire score is then
\[
FF_t = \frac{1}{|T_t|}\sum_{i\in T_t}\mathrm{coord}_i(t),
\qquad
\overline{FF} =
\frac{1}{|\{t:|T_t|>0\}|}
\sum_{t:|T_t|>0} FF_t.
\]
High focus fire means agents often attack the same enemy. However, this should be read as a descriptive signal rather than direct evidence of coordinated decision-making.

To complement this action-level metric, we also measure policy-level diversity with System Neural Diversity~\cite{bettini2024snd}.

\subsection{System Neural Diversity}

System Neural Diversity (SND), introduced by Bettini et al.~\cite{bettini2024snd}, measures how different agents' action distributions are. We compute it in the discrete KAZ action space using Jensen--Shannon (JS) divergence, which is symmetric, bounded in $[0, \log 2]$, and well-defined for categorical distributions.

For each pair of alive agents $(i, j)$, the per-timestep JS divergence is averaged over the episode, then averaged over all pairs:
\[
D_{JS}^{ij} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}
D_{JS}\!\left(\pi_i(\cdot\mid o_i(t)) \,\|\, \pi_j(\cdot\mid o_j(t))\right),
\]
\[
SND = \frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{i<j} D_{JS}^{ij},
\]
where $n$ is the number of agents. High SND signals systematically different action preferences across agents. Low SND means agents behave similarly, either because the policy has not differentiated or because roles genuinely require similar actions.
% Decomposing SND by role pair distinguishes these cases: if archer--knight divergence is substantially higher than within-role divergence, the diversity is structured along role boundaries.

\subsection{Knight Close-Commit Rate}

This metric computes the frequency of close-range engagement decisions by knights. For each alive knight $i$, let $\mathbf{e}_i^\star(t)$ denote the position of its nearest active enemy and define
\[
d_i^\star(t)=\|\mathbf{p}_i(t)-\mathbf{e}_i^\star(t)\|_2.
\]
A \emph{close commit} is recorded when the knight is already near contact range ($105$px) and chooses to move forward or attack:
\[
\mathrm{commit}_i(t)=
\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{alive}_i(t)\land d_i^\star(t)\le 105 \land a_i(t)\in\{1,5\}\}.
\]
The knight close-commit rate is then
\[
\mathrm{KCCR}=
\frac{\sum_t\sum_{i\in K}\mathrm{commit}_i(t)}
{\sum_t\sum_{i\in K}\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{alive}_i(t)\}}.
\]
In simple terms, it measures how often knights go for a nearby enemy when they have the chance.

\section{Protection and Role Balance}

A team can survive and coordinate while still exposing one role to much more risk than the other. This section therefore focuses on direct protection and outcome balance.

\subsection{Unsupported Archer Exposure}
One of the supposed optimal playstyles for archers is to attack from a safer distance, catching stray enemies, rather than being forced into near-contact range without knight cover. For each alive archer $i$ at timestep $t$, let $\mathbf{e}_i^\star(t)$ denote the position of its nearest active enemy and define
\[
d_i^\star(t)=\|\mathbf{p}_i(t)-\mathbf{e}_i^\star(t)\|_2.
\]
Define $\mathrm{supported}_i(t)$ to hold if there exists an alive knight closer to that same enemy by a small support margin $m=5$\,px:
\[
\mathrm{supported}_i(t)\iff \exists\, k \in K_t :
\|\mathbf{p}_k(t)-\mathbf{e}_i^\star(t)\|_2 + m < d_i^\star(t).
\]
The archer is marked as \emph{unsupported and exposed} if $d_i^\star(t) < 150$\,px and $\neg \mathrm{supported}_i(t)$. The episode-level rate is
\[
\mathrm{UAER}=
\frac{\sum_t\sum_{i\in A}\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{alive}_i(t)\land d_i^\star(t)<150 \land \neg \mathrm{supported}_i(t)\}}
{\sum_t\sum_{i\in A}\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{alive}_i(t)\}}.
\]
Lower values mean archers spend less of their alive time in dangerous unsupported states. This metric is more mechanistic than a simple action-frequency summary because it measures positional vulnerability directly.

\subsection{Survival Ratio}

The simplest outcome measure is how long each role survives. For an agent class $\mathcal{C}$ (archers or knights), the survival ratio is
\[
\mathrm{SR}_{\mathcal{C}}
=
\frac{1}{T|\mathcal{C}|}
\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}} \mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{alive}_i(t)\}.
\]

This is reported separately for archers and knights, counted as two metrics. A large gap reveals asymmetric survival: if knights die early, archers face shielded zombies they cannot damage, leading to rapid team collapse.

\subsection{Knight HP Bleed Rate}

Survival alone does not show how much damage agents take while still alive. To measure this, we track how quickly knights lose normalized HP over time. For knight $i$, let
\[
h_i(t)=\frac{\mathrm{HP}_i(t)}{\mathrm{HP}^{\max}_i(t)},
\qquad
\Delta^- h_i(t)=\max\!\left(h_i(t)-h_i(t+1),\,0\right).
\]
The knight HP bleed rate is then
\[
\mathrm{BR}_K=
\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\sum_{i\in K}\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{alive}_i(t)\}\,\Delta^- h_i(t)}
{\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\sum_{i\in K}\mathbf{1}\{\mathrm{alive}_i(t)\}}.
\]

This metric measures how quickly knights lose health, on average. Low values mean they take damage more gradually, while high values mean they are being worn down more quickly. This helps determine whether knights ram into enemies or engage more cautiously.

\subsection{Gini Coefficient}
To measure inequality in end-of-episode experience points, we compute the Gini coefficient over the final XP values of the agents that are still alive at episode end:
\[
G =
\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(2i-n-1)x_i}{n\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_j},
\]
where $x_1 \le x_2 \le \dots \le x_n$ are the sorted XP values and $n$ is the number of surviving agents~\cite{ceriani2012origins}.

The Gini coefficient ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (all XP concentrated in one agent). This is a deliberate late-episode metric: it measures how unevenly progression is distributed among the agents that survive to the end, rather than assigning zero XP to dead agents.

\section{Interpreting Metrics Together}
\label{sec:metric_interpretation}

No single metric tells the full story. High clustering with low focus fire means agents stay together without fighting together. High focus fire with high role separation could mean coordinated attacks from distinct positions, or everyone reacting to the same crisis. Low unsupported archer exposure only looks good when accompanied by strong knight survival, not by archers refusing to engage. The analysis in Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis} reads metrics together rather than trusting any single scalar alone.

\chapter{Training and Experiments}
\label{chap:training}

With the environment, reward schemes, and metrics all in place, it is time to start training. This chapter covers the training setup, experimental protocol, and the hypotheses that guide the analysis.

\section{Training Configuration}

All agents are trained with MAPPO in the JAX-based environment. The primary setup uses \emph{type-shared} parameter sharing, where all agents of the same role share a single network. Two additional parameter-sharing variants are also trained: \emph{non-shared} (independent networks per agent) and \emph{fully shared} (one network for all agents regardless of role). The second variable is the agent's \emph{vision radius}: the default limits each agent's observation to entities within 400\,px, while a comparison condition removes this limit and gives agents full-map visibility.

The actor and critic are both three-layer MLPs with 256 hidden units and tanh activations. The actor outputs 6 action logits, the critic takes the concatenated global state and outputs a scalar value estimate.

Hyperparameters are fixed across all reward schemes (Table~\ref{tab:hyperparams}). The goal is to isolate the effect of reward design, not to tune each policy individually. Each of the seven policies from Section~\ref{sec:reward_design} is trained with \emph{five predetermined seeds} (42, 7, 999, 123, 456) to control for training variance.

After the 10\,000th update, 500 deterministic episodes are run and all metrics computed, producing mean and standard deviation estimates that for the basis of Chapter~\ref{chap:analysis}. Across the main analysis axes, this yields 35 evaluated configuration groups and 175 selected run artifacts for plotting and replay inspection.

\section{Hypotheses}

These hypotheses follow from the reward mechanisms in Section~\ref{sec:reward_design} and from prior work on reward shaping in multi-agent systems~\cite{leibo2017ssd}. The first five address reward design and environment mechanics; the last two address the architectural comparison axes.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Reward design selects coordination style, not just performance.} Different incentive structures -- proximity bonuses, reward sharing, territorial ownership -- should produce measurably different spatial and behavioral regimes, even when final episode lengths are comparable. \textit{Coalition} should produce a compact local regime, \textit{Territorial} should push agents toward divided map usage, and \textit{Shared Reward} should differ from the \textit{Baseline} in spacing and role burden.

  \item \textbf{Equality incentives reduce XP inequality but may hurt durability.} The \textit{Egalitarian} policy should produce the lowest Gini coefficient, but forcing redistribution might reduce total team output and weaken the protective role structure needed for survival.

  \item \textbf{Survival pressure produces the strongest role-protective structure.} The \textit{Survival} policy should develop the clearest frontline-backline separation with high survival ratios, possibly at the cost of a slower, more selective attack tempo.

  \item \textbf{Surface coordination proxies do not predict robustness.} The strongest policies should not simply maximize attack frequency, focus fire, or other immediately intuitive coordination indicators. Instead, durable regimes should combine sensible pacing with strong knight-side protection.

  \item \textbf{Coordination structure depends on environment mechanics, not just reward.} Changing the role-dependency rules -- such as allowing archers to break shields -- should shift which coordination regime performs best, even with identical reward functions.

  \item \textbf{Limited vision promotes structured local cooperation.} Agents with restricted vision (400\,px radius) should develop stronger local or formation-based behavior than agents with full-map visibility.

  \item \textbf{Type-shared parameters enable role specialization.} Type-shared networks should produce stronger role differentiation and better performance than fully shared networks, because the architecture allows different roles to develop different policies while still benefiting from within-role generalization.
\end{enumerate}

Figures~\ref{fig:final-core-summary} and~\ref{fig:metrics-grid} summarize the final deterministic evaluations. The question now is what coordination regime each reward design produced.

In performance terms, Survival leads at 701.1 steps with the lowest seed variance in the set ($\pm$6.0). Zero-sum trails at 404.7. The full ranking is in Table~\ref{tab:episode-length-summary}. More interesting than the ranking itself is that these policies do not converge to a single solution. Different reward functions produce genuinely different coordination styles, and several of them work.

\paragraph{Survival}\mbox{}\\
Survival is the most structured policy. It has the highest formation score and role separation in the set, with both survival ratios near their maximum and the lowest unsupported archer exposure. Yet it does not maximize pressure -- archer attack frequency is only 0.272 and knight HP bleed stays at 0.073. Its Gini coefficient is the highest (0.223), so late-episode XP concentrates among a subset of the surviving agents. In practice, knights absorb pressure up front while archers fire from behind, and the team picks fights rather than maximizing attack volume.

\paragraph{Coalition and Shared Reward}\mbox{}\\
Coalition and Shared Reward contrast sharply. Coalition is the most compact policy with the highest clustering, lowest role separation, and by far the longest coalition lifetime median (51.7 steps), so the groupings are persistent. It has the lowest knight HP bleed and the formation score is only 0.619, so archers are commonly in front. One caveat is that the Coalition bonus uses the same 200\,px distance as the clustering metric, so this particular metric is intentionally aligned with the incentive rather than fully independent of it.

Shared Reward is the opposite: low clustering, high formation, and the highest knight close-commit rate. The archers are often separated shooting from the bottom, while knights are more aggressive near the top.
These are two different styles of play but with similar performance -- Coalition relies on density and temporal cohesion, while Shared Reward is more spatially distinct and aggressive.

\paragraph{Baseline and Zero-Sum}\mbox{}\\
Baseline is the control and performs relatively well, Zero-Sum on the other hand performs substantially worse and shifts into a regime with low formation and role separation and high clustering. Unsupported archer exposure spikes, indicating the archers competing for kills and knights not being protective over them, though the policy is still relatively compact.

\paragraph{Egalitarian and Territorial}\mbox{}\\
Egalitarian and Territorial serve as counterexamples, where policies do achieve the targeted design goal but with weaker performance. Egalitarian has the lowest late-episode Gini coefficient by a wide margin, but also the worst archer survival ratio. Forced equalization leaves ranged agents idle rather than firing from protected positions, breaking the durable role structure that the stronger policies rely on. 
Territorial tries to split the arena into two role-exclusive zones, which are initially followed, but this incentive conflicts with shielded zombies spawning across the full arena, so knights are forced to act on the other side, breaking the formation and ultimately spiraling towards confusion. This can also be seen in the policy's highest focus fire, yet the shortest episodes, highest unsupported archer exposure, and elevated knight HP bleed.

\section{Vision radius comparison}
\label{sec:vision-comparison}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{obrazky-figures/vision_comparison_all_metrics.pdf}
  \caption[Vision radius comparison]{Bar comparison of limited (400\,px) and unlimited vision across the seven policies.}
  \label{fig:vision-comparison}
\end{figure}

Each of the seven policies was also trained with unlimited vision (full-map visibility) under the same type-shared setup, to see whether restricting the observation window actually changes the learned coalition style.

Looking at the episode length ranking in Table~\ref{tab:episode-length-summary}, for four of seven policies, limited vision produces better performance; only Shared Reward $(608.8\to666.4)$ Territorial $(431.7\to474.3)$ and Zero-Sum $(404.7\to421.7)$ benefit from full-map visibility. Among the five that decline, Survival shows the largest drop ($-117$ steps), while the Baseline and Egalitarian decline moderately. Why does more information about the environment reduce performance in many cases? It suggests that in KAZ, full-map visibility might simply add noise that distracts policies from handling the most important local decisions.

\paragraph{Behavior differences}\mbox{}\\
Figure~\ref{fig:vision-comparison} reveals interesting results for how the metrics differ. Coalition is the most dramatic case. Under unlimited vision, it \emph{reverses} its defining compact style: formation score jumps from 0.619 to 0.932, role separation rises from 210 to 627\,px, and clustering drops from 0.593 to 0.296. When Coalition agents can see the entire map, they stop clustering and spread into a formation-based structure resembling Survival. But this more ``organized'' Coalition actually performs worse (539.3 vs 583.3 steps). The compact style was therefore not a failure to learn formation, but an effective local strategy that depends on limited vision.

Survival retains high formation under both conditions (0.974 vs 0.958), but the 117-step drop with unlimited vision suggests the extra information is just more noise. The policy already has everything it needs within 400\,px.

Shared Reward, which goes the other way, is the clearest case where unlimited vision is more effective. Formation drops (0.927 to 0.676) and clustering rises (0.177 to 0.333), yet performance goes up. This makes sense for a more aggressive policy, where seeing more of the map possibly helps agents react and target enemies earlier.

\label{sec:param-sharing-comparison}

Each policy was also trained under three parameter-sharing settings, mentioned in Section~\ref{subsec:param-sharing}: type-shared (TS, the primary setup), non-shared (SEP, independent networks per agent), and fully shared (SH, one network for all agents). The Table~\ref{tab:episode-length-summary} reveals that type-shared produces the longest episodes for all seven policies. The gap is largest for Survival (TS~701.1 vs SEP~509.8 vs SH~504.3), with Coalition and Baseline showing the same pattern. The most architecture-robust policy is Shared Reward, where its fully shared variant comes close to type-shared (SH~585.5 vs TS~608.8).

\paragraph{Failure modes of the alternatives}\mbox{}\\
Figure~\ref{fig:param-sharing-comparison} suggests that non-shared networks sometimes retain or even increase visible structure, for example in formation score (Baseline SEP~0.916 vs TS~0.842), but it does not translate into longer survival. Interestingly enough, fully shared networks achieve almost no XP inequality across agents, with Gini coefficients near zero across most policies, though they perform worst overall. This suggests that one shared network suppresses role specialization.
Survival is the clearest example of this effect. Under type-shared parameters it reaches near-perfect formation (0.974) and the longest episodes in the study, but under both alternatives it drops back to the middle of the pack.

\section{Environment modification: archers break shields}
\label{sec:archers-break-shields}

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{obrazky-figures/archers_break_shields_comparison_all_metrics.pdf}
  \caption[Archers break-shields comparison]{Bar comparison of the archers-break-shields environment modification across all seven evaluated policies.}
  \label{fig:archers-break-shields-comparison}
\end{figure}

So far, knight survival has been the strongest predictor of episode length across the primary evaluation. But that finding depends on an environment rule: only knights can destroy zombie shields. To test how much of the coordination structure actually rests on this asymmetry, we trained all seven policies in a modified environment where archer projectiles can also break shields and receive the standard shield-break reward. Everything else stays identical to the primary setup. The episode length rankings are listed in Table~\ref{tab:episode-length-summary} and Figure~\ref{fig:archers-break-shields-comparison} illustrates the final evaluation metrics bar charts.

\paragraph{Performance shifts}\mbox{}\\
Shared Reward now leads at 668.3 steps, Coalition rises to 633.8, and Survival falls sharply from 701.1 to 572.9,  tying Baseline at 571.6. Zero-Sum improves materially $(404.7\to451.2)$. The most surprising change comes from Territorial: Territorial remains below most policies in the ABS setting, but gains 93.5 steps, the largest improvement in the set. Egalitarian barely moves ($552.8\to551.8$), so relaxing the mechanic does not help every reward equally.

\paragraph{Policy-specific shifts}\mbox{}\\
Shared Reward and Coalition both move even more toward a more attack-oriented play style. Coalition's clustering rises from 0.593 to 0.770, role separation falls from 210 to 144\,px, focus fire nearly triples (0.033~$\to$~0.089), and archer attack frequency rises from 0.213 to 0.281. Shared Reward shows the same: role separation drops from 527 to 391\,px, clustering rises from 0.177 to 0.278, focus fire doubles (0.041~$\to$~0.084), and it becomes the best-performing policy in the modified environment even though archer attack frequency falls (0.666~$\to$~0.547). Supposedly, once archers can help clear shields, compact pressure becomes directly useful for these regimes. 

Baseline moves in the opposite direction: clustering drops from 0.227 to 0.138 while formation rises from 0.841 to 0.938 and unsupported archer exposure falls from 0.053 to 0.018. And Survival keeps its separated frontline-backline signature (formation 0.974~$\to$~0.957, role separation 630~$\to$~603\,px), but loses its advantage because the old knight bottleneck is gone, so the policy keeps doing its specialized knight front-line, but now with less payoff.

Territorial benefits the most in raw episode length. Once archers can break shields in their own lane, the intended split becomes more viable: role separation increases from 402 to 478\,px, focus fire rises from 0.132 to 0.178, and archer attack frequency from 0.322 to 0.409. Knight survival actually worsens (0.756~$\to$~0.652) and it still remains last, but it does overall suggest that knight-breakable shields had been one of the main reasons its left-right ownership scheme failed.

Egalitarian drives its Gini coefficient even lower (0.033~$\to$~0.012) and pushes archers to participate more aggressively, but archer survival falls sharply (0.611~$\to$~0.438) and no durable protective structure emerges. With archers no longer needing knights to break shields, competition becomes easier: Zero-Sum formation increases $(0.593\to0.723)$, role separation increases $(318\to402)$\,px, and archer survival improves, meaning that archers no longer need to stick as close to knights and can more rely on their shield-breaking arrows.

\paragraph{Correlation structure}\mbox{}\\
We also reran correlation analysis and across all 70 runs, knight survival remains the strongest positive correlate of episode length ($\rho = 0.772$), but weaker than in the primary evaluation ($\rho = 0.827$), while knight HP bleed remains strongly negative ($\rho = -0.720$).

Focus fire no longer acts as a uniformly harmful signal. Its overall correlation is still negative $(\rho = -0.398)$, but within more tightly packed Coalition and Shared Reward it becomes positive $(\rho = 0.661)$ and $0.697$, whereas within more spaced out policies like Survival and Egalitarian it remains strongly negative $(\rho = -0.842)$ and $(-0.818)$. Which makes sense, because all are relatively high-performing  policies, so agents in Coalition policy, moving very close together would have naturally better focus fire, whereas Survival, which thrives in separation, naturally has a lower one.

The overall takeaway is that the same policy can produce different spatial organizations under different environment rules. What counts as the ``best'' coalition style depends on what the environment actually requires, so reward design alone does not determine it.

\section{Hypothesis evaluation}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Reward design selects coordination style, not just performance.} The results support this hypothesis. Coalition has the highest clustering (0.593), Survival the clearest formation (0.974), and Shared Reward sits between them with an aggressive spatially-spread style. All three finish in the top four, but through genuinely different regimes. Territorial does push agents toward divided map usage
    as predicted, though the regime collapses under shielded zombie pressure. 

    \item \textbf{Equality incentives reduce inequality but may hurt durability.} Yes, Egalitarian achieves the lowest late-episode Gini coefficient (0.033) but the worst archer survival (0.611). Forced equalization leaves archers idle rather than firing from protected positions, breaking the role structure that other policies rely on.

    \item \textbf{Survival pressure produces the strongest role-protective structure.}
    Correct. Survival leads at 701.1 steps through selective engagement. It has the highest formation score, highest role separation, and lowest unsupported archer exposure, while knight HP bleed stays at 0.073 and archer attack frequency at only 0.272. The leave-one-out ablation strengthens this: Survival loses 451 steps when a single knight is removed, the largest drop of any policy, confirming that the frontline-backline structure really is load-bearing rather than incidental.

    \item \textbf{Surface coordination proxies do not predict robustness.}
    Yes. Focus fire correlates negatively with episode length ($-0.795$). Territorial has the highest focus fire but short episodes. SND is also negatively associated. After removing between-policy variance, spatial metrics lose all significance. Cross-play added more evidence, where almost all mixed-team pairings performed worse than self-play, showing that these regimes are not just interchangeable approximations of one solution. Leave-one-out also confirms severe knight-side burden, meaning one of the biggest predictors for policy success are knights.

    \item \textbf{Coordination structure depends on environment mechanics, not just reward.} Yes. When archers gain shield-breaking ability and the corresponding reward, Shared Reward rises to 668.3, Coalition to 633.8 steps, Territorial gains 93.5 steps, while Survival falls to 572.9 and loses its lead. The same reward functions produce different spatial organizations under different environment rules. Knight survival remains the strongest correlate but weaker (0.772 vs.\ 0.827), and focus fire becomes regime-dependent.

    \item \textbf{Limited vision promotes structured local cooperation.}
    Mostly. Four of seven policies perform better with limited vision. Coalition being the clearest: with unlimited vision it reverses its compact style entirely, spreading into a formation-based structure that actually performs worse. Though, Shared-Reward, Territorial and Zero-Sum gain a real boost from full-map visibility.

    \item \textbf{Type-shared parameters enable role specialization.}
    Yes. Type-shared outperforms both alternatives for all seven policies. The gap is largest for Survival (701.1 vs.\ 509.8 vs.\ 504.3) and Coalition. Non-shared keeps some visible structure, but underperforms. Fully shared networks suppress role specialization, achieving near-zero Gini coefficients across most policies while performing worst overall.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{chap:conclusion}
This thesis asked whether different reward functions produce different coordination structures in a small heterogeneous MARL team, and whether those structures can be meaningfully distinguished beyond reward curves. The answer to both is yes, with several important caveats.

Seven reward schemes were trained in a custom JAX-based KAZ environment using MAPPO primarily with type-shared parameters. The resulting analysis found distinct coordination archetypes, such as Survival's separated frontline-backline formation, Coalition's dense mixed-role cluster, and Shared Reward's aggressive spatially spread style. Each achieves competitive performance through a different mechanism. Several of the intuitive coordination, spatial cohesion, and behavioral metrics turn out to describe which regime a policy occupies rather than how durable it is. The strongest predictor across all regimes is knight survival, and more broadly, how well the team manages frontline burden.

The archers-break-shields modification and leave-one-out ablation support this interpretation more strongly than correlation alone. When archers were allowed to break shields, Shared Reward became the best policy, Coalition also improved, Territorial improved sharply, and Survival lost its lead. Zero-Sum also improved, suggesting that its competitive tendencies made archers less reliant on knights once shield-breaking was available to them, leading to an overall improvement. Knight survival still remained the strongest correlate, but the advantage of high knight-centered separation weakened. Removing a knight caused far larger performance drops than removing an archer across all seven leave-one-out policies. These results show that the coordination structure is shaped jointly by reward design and environment mechanics, and changing either one can shift which regime works best.

On the architectural side, type-shared parameters outperformed both alternatives for all seven policies, and limited vision (400\,px) helped four out of seven. More information does not always help; in several cases it appears to add noise that distracts from the local decisions that actually matter.

\section{Limitations}

The study has several limitations that should be kept in mind when interpreting the results.

The team consists of four agents of two types. This is enough to observe coordination regimes but too small for meaningful coalition partitioning in the game-theoretic sense. Whether the same reward-regime relationships hold at larger scales is an open question.

All findings come from a single KAZ environment, which has specific properties, such as asymmetric roles, descending enemies, and shield mechanics that clearly shape what emerges. Different environments with different interdependence structures could produce entirely different relationships between reward design and coordination.

We deliberately used fixed hyperparameters across all reward schemes to isolate the effect of reward design, but this could mean that some policies may be undertrained relative to what they could achieve with per-policy tuning. Also, we used only one algorithm; different algorithms could push policies to learn different coordination structures under the same rewards.

The metrics are post-hoc and descriptive. They characterize what happened in evaluation trajectories, not \emph{why} agents coordinate the way they do. The replay browser helped bridge this gap by providing frame-by-frame gameplay context that grounded the metric interpretations in the analysis, but it remains a qualitative tool. The ablation experiments go further than metrics alone, but they still cannot fully explain the learning dynamics.

\section{Future Work}
Scaling the team to more agents and introducing additional roles would test whether the coordination archetypes observed here generalize or whether new ones appear. Larger teams would also make genuine coalition partitioning possible.

Testing the same reward schemes in a different cooperative environment would help separate which findings are KAZ-specific and which reflect more general properties of reward-conditioned coordination.

Adding explicit communication channels and comparing them to the implicit coordination found here would help clarify whether the observed structure is driven more by behavioral conventions or by learned communication.
